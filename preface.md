## Preface

### Listening for the Early Church

Until recently, I had never heard of the *Odes of Solomon*. They came to me as part of a longer journey I have been taking into the writings of the early Church—not primarily as a scholar, but as someone trying to listen. I have wanted to move closer to those first centuries of Christian faith and hear voices that have become unfamiliar to many of us.

There is, of course, enormous value in studying these texts historically and critically. Scholars ask when they were written, what language they were composed in, what communities produced them, and how their theology developed. Those questions matter, and without that work many of these ancient writings would scarcely be accessible to us. But they are not the questions that first draw me to these texts.

I want to know what faith felt like.

I want to know how those early Christians imagined the presence of God, how they experienced Christ, what salvation felt like from within, and what images came to them when they prayed. I want to hear their wonder before centuries of theological language made certain expressions familiar to us. As much as the distance of time allows, I want not merely to understand what they believed, but to feel something of what they felt.

That desire eventually led me to the *Odes of Solomon*.

### Songs from an Ancient Church

Despite their traditional name, the *Odes of Solomon* were almost certainly not written by Solomon. They are a collection of forty-two ancient hymns or spiritual poems whose author—or authors—remain unknown. Their precise date is debated, but they are generally placed somewhere around the end of the first century or the early second century, within the formative generations of Christianity.

The collection survives principally in Syriac, though portions are preserved in other ancient languages, and scholars continue to debate its original language and setting. We do not know exactly where these songs were first sung, whether they belonged to a particular worshiping community, or whether one person composed the whole collection. Their origins remain partly hidden from us.

But the voice that emerges from them is unmistakably alive.

The world of the Odes is filled with Christ, Spirit, light, living water, truth, resurrection, divine love, new birth, and participation in the life of God. Their language sometimes feels close to the Gospel of John: Christ is not simply someone to believe propositions about, but Life to be received, Light in which to awaken, and Truth in which to dwell.

Salvation in these songs is wonderfully tangible. It is water flowing into thirsty ground. It is milk given to a child, honey dripping from a honeycomb, a wreath blooming upon the head, a garment being put on, a tree taking root, a river crossed, light entering the darkness. Divine life is something tasted, worn, inhabited, and received.

These are not merely ideas about God.

They are songs sung from inside an encounter with God.

### Whose Voice Is Speaking?

As I worked my way through the Odes, I began noticing something strange. The identity of the speaker sometimes seemed to change.

In one moment, I seemed to be hearing the voice of an ordinary worshiper. The speaker stretches out his hands toward God, cries out in prayer, receives the Spirit, drinks living water, or praises the Lord for deliverance. The relationship seems familiar: a human being speaking to God.

Then, sometimes without warning, the voice changes.

The "I" begins to sound like Christ.

This becomes unmistakable in the final Ode. The speaker stretches out his hands upon the wood, is rejected, descends into Sheol, confronts Death, and discovers that Death cannot hold him. The dead gather around him and cry, "Son of God, have pity on us!" He hears them, sets his name upon them, and calls them free children.

Yet the Odes do not always announce these transitions. There are no convenient stage directions telling us that the human worshiper has stopped speaking and Christ has begun. At other moments Wisdom, Steadfast Love, the Spirit, or divine Speech seems to move through the song.

My modern instinct was to sort out the voices.

Eventually, I began to wonder whether I was supposed to.

### When the Voices Mingle

There is an intimacy within the *Odes of Solomon* that resists the clean boundaries I wanted to impose upon them. Christ is not merely someone standing outside the worshiper, being observed from a distance. His life enters human life. His Spirit rests upon the worshiper. His light shines from within. His words find human lips.

The distinction between Christ and the human person does not disappear, but communion becomes so intimate that their voices sometimes seem to mingle.

One line from the final Ode captures this beautifully:

> "I will speak by their mouths."

Perhaps that is one way of understanding what happens throughout these songs.

The worshiper sings of Christ until, almost imperceptibly, Christ seems to sing through the worshiper. The human person speaks of divine life and then discovers that divine life is speaking from within. Prayer becomes participation.

This is part of what makes the Odes feel so mystical. Their Christianity is not merely a matter of believing the correct things about God. It is a life of indwelling and communion. Christ is encountered, received, inhabited, and expressed.

The singer remains the singer.

And yet another Voice sings through the song.

### Letting the Song Continue

That realization shaped the way I approached the chapters in this book.

These are not new translations of the *Odes of Solomon*, nor are they intended as scholarly commentaries. They are contemplative expansions. I have taken the images and movements of the ancient songs and tried to remain with them long enough for them to open.

When an Ode offered living water, I followed the water. When it spoke of light, I remained in the light. When it gave me trees, roots, milk, honey, crowns, rivers, clouds, garments, or embraces, I tried not to hurry past the image in search of an explanation. I allowed the image itself to become prayer.

I have also resisted the temptation to resolve every ambiguity of voice. When the worshiper's voice seems to become the voice of Christ, I have generally allowed the transition to happen without explanation. The ambiguity belongs to the beauty of these songs. To draw hard lines everywhere might make them easier to analyze while diminishing something of their intimacy.

The chapters that follow, then, should be read as invitations rather than explanations. The ancient Odes remain what they have always been. These pages simply linger within them.

### The Song Continues

I invite you to read them slowly.

Some images will feel immediately familiar. Others may seem strange. Let them remain strange for a while. Before turning living water into a theological concept, drink from it. Before explaining the light, stand within it. Before defining what it means to participate in Christ, listen for the moment when you are no longer certain whether the worshiper is singing to Christ or Christ is singing through the worshiper.

Nearly two thousand years separate us from the people who first sang these songs. We do not know their names. We do not know the rooms in which they gathered or what their voices sounded like when these words were still new.

Their mouths have long since fallen silent.

But the songs did not.

They crossed languages, centuries, forgotten libraries, and fragile manuscripts until they reached us. Now we receive them into our own breath and allow them to become prayer again.

Perhaps this is another fulfillment of that ancient line:

*"I will speak by their mouths."*

May we listen until the song finds ours.
