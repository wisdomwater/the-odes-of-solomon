## 2 – Immortal Land

I saw trees beautiful and fruitful, standing together in a land untouched by decay. Their branches were heavy with life, their leaves full and open, and upon them rested wreaths that no human hand had woven. They had not adorned themselves, nor had anyone climbed among their branches to place glory upon them; self-grown was their wreath, rising naturally from the life that moved within them.

Their timber flourished, and their fruits rejoiced. Even the wood seemed alive with gladness, as though joy had entered the grain and traveled from root to branch, from branch to leaf, from leaf to fruit. Nothing strained to become what it was meant to be. The trees simply received life, and because they received it deeply, they became fruitful.

I have known another way of living. I have tried to force fruit from branches before their season, to make myself fruitful by effort, to prove that I am alive by what I can produce. I have decorated barren places and called them flourishing, fastening wreaths upon myself that looked beautiful for a little while but had no root from which to draw life.

But these trees wore no borrowed glory.

Their roots were from immortal land.

I looked beneath all that could be seen—the spreading branches, the ripening fruit, the crown of leaves—and understood that the secret was hidden below. Their beauty began in darkness, where roots reached quietly into a soil that death could not exhaust. What appeared above the earth was only the visible consequence of an invisible communion.

So much of my life is lived among visible things. I see what has grown and what has failed, what others have accomplished and what I have not, which branches are heavy with fruit and which remain bare. Yet the life of God draws my attention downward, beneath appearance and comparison, toward the hidden place where the roots drink.

There I do not need to perform.

There I need only remain.

The roots do not argue with the earth. They do not apologize for their hunger or feel ashamed of their dependence. They descend because they were made to receive, stretching through the darkness until they find what has always been waiting for them.

And beneath them flows the river of joy.

It waters the trees and passes around the land of their eternal life, moving without exhaustion, giving without becoming empty. I hear it before I see it, a quiet movement beneath everything, a current deeper than fear and older than sorrow. I had imagined joy as something fragile, a brief brightness arriving when circumstances were kind, but this joy is a river.

It does not depend upon the weather.

Storms may bend the branches. Seasons may alter what can be seen. There may be days when the sky is dark and the leaves tremble beneath winds they cannot command, yet beneath them the river continues to move through immortal land.

I lower my roots toward it.

There are places within me that have become dry from striving, places hardened by disappointment and guarded by fear. I have sometimes mistaken this dryness for my true nature, as though thirst were proof that there was no water. Yet thirst itself becomes a kind of knowledge: something in me remembers the river.

So I descend through my own hunger.

I pass beneath the identities I have constructed and the wreaths I have tried to weave for myself. I pass beneath success and failure, beneath praise and rejection, beneath the anxious question of whether I have become enough. Deeper still is the life I did not create, the life that was given before I knew how to ask for it.

The river is there.

Its waters do not accuse my thirst. They meet it. They enter the hidden roots and travel slowly upward, carrying life into places I thought had withered beyond recovery.

What God gives below begins to appear above. Patience grows where anger once hardened the branch. Mercy ripens where judgment had been bitter, courage where fear had narrowed the heart, and compassion where suffering once curved everything inward. The fruit rejoices because it is no longer being manufactured; it has become the natural abundance of a life rooted in another Life.

Perhaps this is what holiness feels like from within—not the anxious construction of a flawless self, but the flowering of communion. I do not become radiant by fastening light upon myself. I become radiant by remaining where the light can enter me, just as the tree becomes fruitful by remaining rooted where the river runs.

And the river does not stop with one tree.

It moves around the whole land of eternal life, touching root after root beneath the soil. What appears above as many trees is joined below by the same generous water. Each bears its own branches and its own fruit, yet none possesses the river.

This loosens something in me. I do not need another tree to wither so that I may flourish, nor must another's fruit diminish for mine to become sweet. Divine abundance has no need of rivalry, for the river loses nothing by watering us both.

Let my roots, then, grow toward communion rather than possession. Let the life I receive become shelter rather than superiority, fruit rather than pride. If joy rises through me, let someone weary rest beneath its branches; if healing ripens upon me, let it become food for another.

For fruit is not given to the tree for the tree alone.

The river of joy enters secretly, but it does not remain secret forever. It becomes leaf and shade, fragrance and sweetness, strength in the timber and nourishment in the fruit. The invisible gift takes visible form, and what began as receiving becomes giving.

Blessed are those who see Your waters.

Yet I wonder whether seeing begins with thirst. Perhaps the eyes open when I finally cease pretending that dry ground can sustain me. Perhaps I recognize the river when I admit that every wreath I weave for myself will eventually fade, and that only what grows from immortal land can share in immortal life.

So bring me again to the river.

Let me sit beside waters that do not run dry and listen until their movement becomes quieter than my striving and deeper than my fear. Let my roots find their way through every hardened place until they touch that hidden current, and let Your joy rise within me without force.

May it enter the roots.

May it fill the timber.

May it reach the branches.

May the fruit rejoice.

And when I look upon the life growing there, keep me from imagining that I made it. Let me remember the dark soil, the unseen roots, and the river moving faithfully beneath them all.

For my roots are in immortal land, and beneath everything, Your waters are still flowing.
