## Epilogue

### The Song Continues

We have traveled through a strange and beautiful landscape.

We have drunk living water and tasted honey. We have stood beneath clouds of peace, watched trees send their roots deep into the earth, crossed raging rivers upon footprints that remained above the waters, and heard Steadfast Love calling humanity away from the paths of destruction. Finally, we followed Christ into the depths of Sheol and heard the astonishing declaration that Death itself could not contain him.

Through all these images, one conviction returns again and again: **God gives life.**

Darkness is real in the Odes. So are oppression, deception, fear, suffering, and death. These songs do not pretend otherwise. But none of these things possesses the deepest reality. Light enters darkness. Truth exposes deception. Steadfast Love descends into destruction. Life enters Death itself, and Death discovers that it has swallowed something it cannot keep.

When I began reading these songs, I wanted to know something of what the early Church felt. I wanted to move behind the theological systems that developed over the centuries and listen, as much as possible, to the experience of those who lived nearer Christianity's beginnings.

I expected to learn something about what they believed about Christ.

Instead, I found myself wondering how deeply they believed they could **participate in Christ**.

The divine life of the Odes is continually being given away. We drink the water. We put on the garment. We receive the Spirit. We are planted and watered. Our faces begin to shine. Christ speaks through human mouths. Salvation is not merely something that happens somewhere outside us; it enters us and begins transforming what we are.

And what enters us eventually flows outward.

If we have received steadfast love, we become capable of steadfast love. If we have been carried through darkness, perhaps we can enter another person's darkness without fear. If mercy has found us, mercy can begin finding others through us. The water we drink becomes a spring.

This is why the call of Ode 33 remains so important: *Forsake the ways of this Destruction.*

There are always ways of destruction available to us. Contempt, domination, vengeance, fear, and hatred readily offer themselves as tools, sometimes even disguising themselves as righteousness. But the way of Christ is different. He does not defeat destruction by becoming more destructive. Steadfast Love enters the darkness carrying something darkness does not possess.

Perhaps this is how we continue the ancient song.

We become a cloud of peace above someone who is afraid. We become a bridge for someone standing before a raging river. We become a tree whose branches make room for another. We become living water for the thirsty and steadfast love for those who have forgotten that they are loved.

The final Ode gives us one last image. Christ descends into the deepest place, and the dead run toward him. He hears their cry, opens the way, places his name upon their heads, and calls them his own.

He does not emerge from Death alone.

That may be the final movement of all these songs: life received becomes life shared. Light spreads. Water flows. Trees bear fruit. Love descends into darkness and returns carrying others with it.

The Odes have ended, but the song has not.

May the water we have received become a spring within us. May the light by which we have seen begin to shine through us. And wherever Death still whispers that something is beyond redemption, may we remember the final song:

**Death swallowed Life—and could not keep it.**
